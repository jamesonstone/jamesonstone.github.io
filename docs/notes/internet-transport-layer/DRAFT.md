# The Internet was Always Just a Transport Layer

Much of what we call using the internet is operating somebody else's interface to get something out of their database. We search, compare, fill out forms and copy information between systems. The website makes us do the translation.

Agents make that arrangement optional.

The title is a metaphor, not an origin story. The Internet connects networks and processes. The Web made linked information accessible to people. Email and human communication were there early. None of that establishes a requirement that a person visit a page whenever they need something it provides.

Consider buying a replacement part. The useful information is compatibility, availability, delivered price and return terms. Each seller presents those facts through its own navigation, categories and checkout. The buyer must reconstruct one decision from several unrelated interfaces. Much of the browsing is clerical work imposed by the way the information is packaged.

An agent can do some of that reconstruction. Where a service offers an authorized interface, it can query the underlying information directly. Elsewhere, it may operate the same pages a person would. Either way, the person can receive the comparison without personally touring every storefront. A site can remain useful while disappearing from the user's experience.

This gives dead internet theory a more interesting interpretation. Useful activity can continue without a corresponding human audience on every page. That possibility says nothing about whether most current participants are bots or whether anyone coordinated their arrival. It changes what a missing audience means. A network of information and capabilities can function while fewer people attend its individual interfaces.

For a business, that changes what deserves investment. Accurate inventory, intelligible terms, reliable transactions and evidence that a claim is true become more consequential when the customer delegates the visit. Presentation can communicate trust to a person. An agent also needs something it can verify. A polished page cannot compensate for a service that fails to deliver.

Beauty still has its own claim. A mural does not need to shorten a transaction to justify its existence. A room can be worth entering because of how it feels. A website can offer the same value: atmosphere, surprise, argument, contact with another person's taste. Sometimes the visit is what someone wants. Automating it would misunderstand the objective.

Delegation also has limits. An agent needs permission to act, must distinguish a quote from a commitment, and can misunderstand both the source and the user. A purchase still creates obligations. A confident summary does not establish provenance. Replacing a browser with an agent can simply move dependence to a new intermediary with its own incentives.

My expectation is that these uses separate further. We will choose some places for the experience and access other services through whatever interface best serves the task. The underlying network can support both.

The vulnerable assumption is that people must experience your website to benefit from what you provide. For a great deal of the web, the visit was a requirement of the interface. Agents give that requirement something to answer for.
