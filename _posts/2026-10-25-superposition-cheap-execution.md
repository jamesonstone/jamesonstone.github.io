title: "Superposition IV: Cheap Execution, Expensive Frames"

Always-on agents change the opportunity cost of an unexecuted idea. A possibility that once competed for an engineer's working hours can now become work in progress overnight. More ambitions become feasible. Unused execution capacity can consequently begin to feel like a failure of imagination: if the machine can keep working, why has nothing worth doing been assigned to it?

That pressure embeds an objective before it announces itself as a productivity problem. Keeping capacity occupied becomes evidence of seriousness, and a growing workload becomes evidence of ambition. Yet neither establishes that the resulting work deserves to exist. Accepting limits is a substantive challenge here: the demand for perpetual execution may be another inherited premise, not an unavoidable consequence of technical capability.

Cheap execution can also create expensive framing. The first description generates a plan, artifacts, and dependencies. Redirection now requires deciding which of them survive. Their production cost may have been trivial, but the attention required to evaluate and abandon them is not. The interpretation acquires momentum through the obligations it creates.

A partition can be resized, an alert automated, a log populated. Each local success confirms the usefulness of delegation while leaving the decomposition intact. A more encompassing abstraction might ask what operational understanding is actually required: what has changed, what matters, and when intervention is justified. That could dissolve several tasks rather than accelerate their execution.

The scarce capacity is then the ability to change levels of explanation. A list of solvable problems can conceal a single misidentified need. Assigning every item to an agent preserves that mistake at greater throughput. An impressive volume of completed work can coexist with a failure to understand why the work was necessary.

This is where ambition requires a harder test than output. Can the objective itself be revised after execution becomes available? Can a question survive long enough to expose the assumptions that made the task list seem inevitable? A capable agent makes implementation easier to justify. The more consequential achievement may be an abstraction that makes implementation unnecessary.
