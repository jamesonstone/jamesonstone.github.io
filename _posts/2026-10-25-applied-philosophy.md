Applied philosophy already has a history; the [Society for Applied Philosophy](https://www.appliedphil.org/society-for-applied-philosophy-annual-conference-2018/) was founded in 1982. The proposal here is an engineering practice whose material includes the premises themselves. “The engineers that philosophers aren't” names an obligation anyone can assume: responsibility for what an idea makes compulsory.

Applied science can establish how to achieve an objective. Engineering can realize it under constraints. Neither accomplishment establishes the objective's legitimacy. Definitions of success determine what a system rewards, excludes, and permits its measurements to recognize. Treating those definitions as requirements can leave the most consequential design choices beyond scrutiny.

Consider an organization that defines autonomy as the absence of approval. Removing approvals accelerates decisions, apparently validating the design. Yet authority without resources, information, or the ability to refuse a demand can increase responsibility without increasing control. The experiment measured its chosen definition faithfully; it never established that the definition was adequate.

Reconceiving autonomy as power over which objectives can be accepted makes budget authority and the right of refusal constitutive features. Philosophical revision changes what must be built.

Implementation then exposes the premise to consequences an argument could leave abstract. But a system organized around obedience can classify resistance as malfunction and compliance as vindication. Its apparent responsiveness immunizes its objective against criticism.

An engineering practice of philosophy must therefore include the conditions of its own contestability. Who bears the cost? Who can refuse the arrangement, challenge its objective, or require its dismantling? Responsibility cannot reserve revision for the designer while making everyone else experimental material.
