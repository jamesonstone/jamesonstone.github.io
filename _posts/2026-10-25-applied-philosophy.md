Applied philosophy already has a history; the [Society for Applied Philosophy](https://www.appliedphil.org/society-for-applied-philosophy-annual-conference-2018/) was founded in 1982. The proposal here is an engineering practice whose material includes the premises themselves. Applied science can establish how to achieve an objective under particular conditions. Engineering can translate that knowledge into a functioning system. Neither accomplishment establishes the objective's legitimacy, nor exhausts the possible definitions of success. Those questions determine what the system makes possible, rewards, or excludes. Treating them as settled requirements permits extraordinary ingenuity within an impoverished conception of the problem. Applied philosophy would make those commitments available for reconstruction, then assume responsibility for the arrangements they require. “The engineers that philosophers aren't” names that obligation. A philosopher can assume it; an engineer can evade it.

Consider an organization that defines autonomy as the absence of approval. Removing approvals accelerates decisions, and the improvement appears to validate the design. Yet authority without resources, relevant information, or the ability to refuse a demand can increase responsibility without increasing control. The experiment measured the chosen definition faithfully. It did not establish that the definition was adequate. Reconceiving autonomy as power over which objectives can be accepted would change the architecture of the organization: budget authority, access to information, and the right of refusal become constitutive features. The philosophical claim acquires practical obligations, while the resulting arrangements expose omissions in the claim. This is more demanding than attaching an ethical commentary to an otherwise finished design. The concept changes what must be built, and its implementation can change what the concept must mean.

Consequences nevertheless cannot confer automatic legitimacy. A functioning system can instantiate an indefensible premise with exceptional fidelity. Worse, the premise may determine whose experience counts as evidence: a system organized around obedience can classify resistance as malfunction and compliance as vindication. Its apparent responsiveness protects it from correction. Applied philosophy must therefore design the conditions under which its own justification can be contested. Who bears the cost of an idea? Who can refuse its demands, challenge its objective, or require its dismantling? Taking responsibility cannot mean reserving revision for the designer while making everyone else experimental material. The obligation runs in both directions: premises can condemn a successful system, and consequences can reveal a premise's inadequacy. The practice begins where neither is permitted to immunize the other from scrutiny. Want to join?
